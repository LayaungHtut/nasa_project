# Judge tour - approximately 130 seconds

0. **Hook (10s, the tour opens here):** the map is in Monsoon Series mode. The tour plays the timeline automatically: 12 NISAR passes, June to November 2026, through monsoon cloud. Point at the chart: paddy fields darkest on Aug 27 (flooded), then 2.0 dB brighter than forest by Oct 14 as the rice grows. Say: "92% of the 'water loss' a two-date comparison found is on paddy fields. The fields changed, not the reservoir."

1. **Question (10s):** Did the water change, or did its radar signal? Introduce Hlawga Reservoir near Yangon and the value of radar when optical imagery is obscured by clouds.
2. **Observations (15s):** Identify the August 27 and September 8, 2026 GCOV acquisitions. Show the dates and source identifiers. These are observations, not proof that a flood happened between them.
3. **Compare (15s):** Drag the before/after divider, then select the timeline dates. Explain that both previews use the same -15 to +5 dB display range. Switch to HV briefly: it is brighter than HH and streaked, and fails our quality checks, so the analysis uses HH.
4. **Candidates (15s):** Select Change Candidates. The configured algorithm flags 0.056 km² across 556 pixels. Its 0.639 score is a heuristic, not a probability. Show the 3 x 3 filter and -9.54 dB threshold.
5. **Investigate (15s):** Disclose the larger 4.517 km² apparent water loss and the parameter sensitivity. On the ESA WorldCover map, 4.149 km² of it is paddy and 0.031 km² reservoir, so it is the rice fields changing, not drawdown. Mention the honest caveats: the Oct 26 / Nov 7 passes use newer processing, and no crop calendar or field survey has confirmed the rice timing.
6. **Drivers (15s):** Select HH Change. Orange brightened, blue darkened. Open "What could be driving the change?". We tested the obvious explanation, wind rippling the reservoir, against airport weather reports: both mornings were near calm, so it is now weakly supported and the cause is open. Rice growth is a possible cause of the brightening paddy belt. Each hypothesis names the record that would test it.
7. **Field by field (15s):** The tour switches to Field Timing. Say: "An average can hide a few loud pixels, so we tested every 70 m patch, with forest as the control. 72% of paddy brightened by more than 1 dB; 4% of forest did." Point at the colours: each field's darkest, most water-like pass. Timing forms patches (67% neighbour agreement at 150 m vs 50% for random labels), which fits fields planted at different times. A local shower could also make patches, so planting dates from farmers would settle it.
8. **Understand (10s):** Open the SAR explainer. Smooth water often returns little energy; flooded vegetation can behave differently. The summary is generated from records using a template, not live AI.
9. **Independent check (10s):** Open the dated optical overlays. Clouds prevent a close-date paired check. A wider-date comparison gives 9 land-to-water matches, 101 land-to-land disagreements, and 446 unknown cells. Explain the roughly four-day offsets; keep the candidate unconfirmed. Download the evidence for review.

The tour does not claim confirmed inundation, affected population, or an operational flood-warning service. The synthetic scenario is a separately labeled teaching aid.
